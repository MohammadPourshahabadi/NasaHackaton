How Healthy Astronauts works

On long missions astronauts must spot health changes themselves, often minutes away from Earth by radio. Healthy Astronauts turns a quick daily check-in into a clear answer: how am I compared with my baseline, and what should I do next?

1 · Log it your way

Type a sentence, photograph a device screen, or answer the six-question wellbeing check. A local language model pulls out the readings and the body area. A number is kept only if it appears in what you wrote or on the screen.

2 · Compare with a baseline

Every reading is placed on a bar with its baseline range. Readings outside the range turn red and move to Needs attention.

3 · Act on it

Transparent rules decide green, yellow or red and show the next step with an animation: repeat a measurement correctly, take a nap, or try meditation and music. The AI explains in plain words but never changes the result.

What the NASA and SIM tags mean

NASA

The baseline is based on data published by NASA: Apollo AP005 (heart rate, blood pressure), Apollo AP004 (serum sodium, potassium, creatinine, glucose) and the NASA Standard Measures sleep study. The range is the NASA mean ± 2 standard deviations.

SIM

The baseline is generated (simulated) data. NASA has not published a range for these measures (calf ultrasound area, eye OCT), so each crew member gets a random preflight value inside an approximate normal range. It is clearly not real data.

NASA data used

  • Apollo AP004 Clinical Biochemistry: serum sodium, potassium, creatinine and glucose (preflight mean ± 2 SD, n=33).
  • Apollo AP005 Cardiovascular Evaluations: resting heart rate and blood pressure (preflight supine, n=24).
  • NASA Standard Measures sleep study: in-flight sleep, 7.33 h average.
  • ISS Tele-SUS and NASA Fluid Shifts: measurement methods for calf ultrasound and eye OCT.
  • NASA Spaceflight Standard Measures: topics of the daily wellbeing questions.
